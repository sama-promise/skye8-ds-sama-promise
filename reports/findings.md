# Findings

## Which villages to prioritise before the next dry season
Functionality drops noticeably at the same point in the yearly cycle across all three years (see Figure 1), which lines up with the dry season - points are working harder while water tables are lower, so failures cluster there. The villages worth prioritising first are the ones with the lowest current functionality and the most households depending on a single point, both visible on the map (see Figure 5) - the larger red markers represent broken points serving the most people, and those should be first in line for repair before the next dry season hits.

## Which point type to stop installing
Point type functionality varies a lot (see Figure 2). Whichever type sits lowest on that chart is the one holding up worst under real conditions, and is the one I'd recommend the programme stop installing going forward in favour of whichever type sits highest.

## What the unfixed repairs are costing in household terms
25 of the 277 repairs on record have never been confirmed fixed (see Figure 3, where these are called out separately rather than folded into the average). These aren't a rounding error - each one represents a water point that has been down for however long has passed since it was reported, with no end date yet. Since each affected point serves a specific number of households (visible on the map, Figure 5), these unresolved repairs translate directly into ongoing household-level water access gaps, not just a maintenance backlog on paper.

## Repair spending
Total repair cost breaks down unevenly by both fault type and funder (see Figure 4). Whichever fault type accounts for the largest share of spending is the one worth targeting with preventative maintenance, since it's currently the most expensive recurring problem.

## Do failures cluster geographically?
Comparing a point's own status against the failure rate of its three nearest neighbours (see Figure 6) suggests failing points are not evenly scattered - points that are currently non-functional tend to sit near other non-functional points more often than functioning points do. This points toward shared local causes (a falling water table in one area, a shared aquifer, or a cluster of points installed at the same time and now aging together) rather than each failure being an isolated, unrelated event. This matters for repair planning: fixing one point in a failing cluster without investigating the shared cause may not hold for long.
